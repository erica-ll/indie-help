8. Blue does work, and it's not just a cliché — there's real color-psychology reasoning behind it. Blue conveys stability and comfort, and light blue specifically reads as calming and emotionally relieving (which is why hospitals lean on white/light-blue color schemes, in contrast to the red/yellow schemes fast-food restaurants use to give a fast-paced psychological hint). The "reads cold" risk is real but manageable: pair blue with a warm complementary color and control the ratio between them, the way Naraka: Bladepoint's Kurumi costume mixes blue with orange. It's also worth knowing that color carries a lot of weight in first impressions — roughly 85% of a viewer's snap judgment at the ~20-second mark comes from color versus about 15% from shape. 14. Yes, it's worth proceeding, but the value depends on the specificity being genuine, not decorative. Real cultural specificity tends to generate an audience rather than shrink it — it makes a game more memorable than a generic entry in its genre, and can draw in diaspora audiences, cozy-game players, aesthetic-driven players, and people who specifically seek out short indie games (Venba is cited as a comparable success story built this way). Because this kind of work is personal and drawn from one's own background, it's also worth going in with real psychological safety around how it might be received. 18. Random jitter is a real, directly documented technique from Cocoon's rendering pipeline. When ray-tracing a voxel/light-probe bake, each ray matrix is given a small random directional jitter (about one voxel's worth) combined with a near-plane offset of about one voxel size. Offsetting the ray origin/direction this way specifically prevents self-shadowing artifacts that would otherwise appear when a surface's own geometry incorrectly occludes the ray meant to sample light arriving at that same surface. This is stated as a concrete, applied fix for a named technical problem (self-shadowing during light baking), not a general design philosophy. Source file: GDC - Pondering Orbs The Rendering and Art Tools of 'COCOON'.txt 
19. Yes — angular ray marching, credited by name to Simon Brown. Instead of stepping linearly along the ray (which the speaker demonstrates looks noisy), you step in equal angular increments in the cookie texture's angular domain, which importance-samples the inverse-square falloff and produces much less noise for the same step count. Used in Cocoon for the volumetric "cookie" light effect (e.g. the amber/key light glow). Source file: GDC - Pondering Orbs The Rendering and Art Tools of 'COCOON'.txt

Yes — Grounded (the Obsidian/Xbox game) has an "arachnophobia mode," informally called the "spider slider," that came out of internal user research at Xbox into how arachnophobia actually manifests differently for different people. It's a continuous slider rather than an on/off toggle: at one end is the full spider, and at the other is the "danger dumpling," a heavily de-spiderified version of the same enemy — some players needed it all the way down, others were fine at the halfway point. The key design insight was that the enemy didn't need to be a spider mechanically at all; the combat role (something bigger than you that you have to make decisions around) was separable from its visual presentation, which is what made the slider possible. 24. Not always — it depends on the scope of what you're building. Building your own engine is cost-effective when you keep it simple, since trying to architect your own "epic," full-featured solution carries real, ongoing costs to support it. That inherent cost is why the speaker points to companies like CD Projekt Red moving to Unreal — and says that was probably the right call for them, since their in-house engine was in a very sad spot. Separately, the speaker also names a specific cause behind Cyberpunk 2077's troubled development: the designers were working on a moving target, since the engine was still being actively built while they were trying to make content for it. So there are two distinct points here — building small and simple in-house is cost-effective and often the right choice, while building a large, complex homegrown engine can carry costs that push a studio toward an off-the-shelf engine instead, and, separately, having your engine and your content built simultaneously can itself derail a project.
